Interview proof plan | Three minutes

Project Coordination Walkthrough

A concise, permission-based demonstration that shows readiness to support established project procedures without turning the interview into a presentation or implying prior authority.

Start with the dashboard Review the process map
Presentation boundary Ask permission, show three controls, stop on time.

The case study is supporting evidence, not the center of the interview. The interviewer should remain in control of the conversation.

Use only when relevant: Do not force the walkthrough into the interview. Offer it after discussing documentation, change coordination, invoices, project systems, or closeout.

Permission request

Before sharing
“I prepared a short, fictional, and nonconfidential project-coordination example to show how I think about status, changes, invoice support, and handoffs. Would it be useful if I showed you three screens for about three minutes?”

If the interviewer says no or time is short, continue the conversation normally. The preparation still helps you answer questions with greater specificity.

Timed sequence

The three-minute walkthrough

Use the exact sequence below. Do not open every artifact. Keep the emphasis on your working approach and respect for established procedures.

  1. 0:00 to 0:25

    Set the boundary

    Open the Weekly Coordination Dashboard.

    “This is a reconstructed academic case using synthetic data. It is not meant to copy or replace your process. It shows the coordination discipline I would bring after learning your systems, procedures, and approval structure.”
  2. 0:25 to 1:10

    Show decision-ready status

    Point to the three items requiring attention: concealed corrosion, the incomplete pay application, and the quotation needing clarification.

    “My job as a coordinator is not to make these decisions. It is to make the decision need, owner, impact, support, and deadline visible so the right person can act.”

    What this proves: You understand status reporting, ownership, escalation, and role boundaries.

  3. 1:10 to 1:55

    Show the change-package gate

    Open the Change Package Completeness Control and point to technical scope plus safety and access effects.

    “Before routing a potential change, I would confirm that the source, quotation, cost, time, references, routing, and required supporting records are complete. Here the package stays open because two items still require authorized review.”

    What this proves: You understand change coordination without claiming technical, contractual, or approval authority.

  4. 1:55 to 2:35

    Show the invoice hold

    Open the Invoice Routing Completeness Control and point to certified payroll and the conditional lien release.

    “The values reconcile to the revised commitment, but the package should not enter the approval queue because two required documents are missing. I would record the exact hold reason, owner, and due date, then route it only when complete.”

    What this proves: You can support invoice processing, pay applications, and financial control while respecting approval authority.

  5. 2:35 to 3:00

    Close with fit, not process criticism

    “The formats would change to match your project platforms and procedures. The consistent part is how I work: accurate records, clear ownership, complete routing, early exception visibility, and dependable follow-through.”

    Stop sharing and return the conversation to the interviewer.

Likely follow-up

Questions the walkthrough may invite

Answer from the role boundary and the evidence shown. Do not claim use of a named platform or process that you have not actually used.

“Did you use this on a real project?”

“The case is a portfolio reconstruction based on construction administration coursework and my current document-control and workflow experience. All project data is synthetic. I built it to demonstrate how I now connect the processes.”

“Would you change our process to this?”

“No. My first step would be learning your approved procedure, system, terminology, and approval matrix. I would use these same control principles within that environment.”

“What would you do when a package is incomplete?”

“I would document the missing item, current owner, due date, impact, and routing gate, notify the appropriate parties, and keep the item visible until the requirement is satisfied or an authorized exception is documented.”

“How do you avoid overstepping?”

“I separate preparation and completeness checks from technical interpretation, commercial decisions, payment approval, and owner authorization. I document who has authority for each step.”

“What is the process-improvement element?”

“Small controls: one identifier, one owner, one due date, explicit status definitions, missing-document flags, linked records, and clear escalation. Those improve reliability without replacing the established workflow.”

“How would this work in our project system?”

“I would map the required fields and statuses to your existing platform, then follow your naming, permission, routing, and reporting standards. The examples are platform-neutral on purpose.”

Presentation discipline

What not to do

The strongest impression comes from judgment and restraint, not from showing the largest number of documents.

Do

  • Ask permission before sharing.
  • State that the case is reconstructed and synthetic.
  • Show only three screens.
  • Connect each screen to the job duties.
  • Emphasize established procedures and authority.
  • Stop at three minutes.

Do not

  • Say the employer should adopt your templates.
  • Critique their current systems before understanding them.
  • Claim experience with tools you have not used.
  • Present yourself as an approver, engineer, or contract authority.
  • Open every file in the evidence pack.
  • Continue after the interviewer signals they want to move on.

Thirty-second version

Use this when there is not enough time to share screens:

“I built a synthetic project-coordination case study to sharpen how I connect commitments, meetings, changes, invoices, and closeout. The central principle is that I prepare and verify complete records, make owners and deadlines visible, and escalate exceptions, while authorized staff retain technical, contractual, and payment decisions. I would apply that discipline within your established procedures and systems.”
